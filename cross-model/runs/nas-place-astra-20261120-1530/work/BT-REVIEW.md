# Using bt on the four-drive CM4 carrier

I used bt throughout this 254-footprint board. Its largest contributions were connecting individual pads to planes, routing the ordinary signals after the fast channels were protected, and checking changes against KiCad. The final board combines bt-generated copper, paired and detailed routes generated separately, and KiCad zone fills. Counts below are approximate where I did not keep a complete command history. The times and routing counts quoted for individual runs come from their output.

## Where it helped

| Commands | Use on this job | Result |
|---|---|---|
| `board`, `rules`, `part`, `query`, `ratsnest` | One full inventory, several rule reads, roughly two dozen part and area lookups, and starting connectivity checks | The complete board dump made a searchable pin-to-net inventory. Saving it and then querying a specific pin or box kept subsequent outputs short. UUIDs from `query` were particularly useful when shifting copper around the crystal and jack. |
| `render` | About twenty renders, including rejected trials | A box around the switch or service controller was much easier to review than a full-board image at this density. The same command produced repeatable visual checkpoints. I used KiCad’s renderer for the final surface views. |
| `plane`, `via --near` | Roughly a dozen rail and ground passes and several individual via searches | One GND pass reported 252 of 268 pads served in two seconds. The via coordinates, shared vias and reasons for failure saved checking hundreds of sites individually. I still refilled and verified the islands in KiCad. |
| `route --nets`, `route --net` | One major batch and several targeted attempts | The short-first batch on F.Cu and B.Cu with a 0.10 mm grid and two seconds per search connected 95 of 135 attempted nets in 282 seconds. It returned 718 segments and 95 vias. The warning about walling in U12.26 named the specific pin that needed another escape. |
| `probe`, `apply`, `fill` | Repeated staging and checking, on the order of two dozen applications and multiple refills | `-o` made it practical to throw away a bad route. `apply --checked` independently accepted the 718-segment batch in one step. The distinction between generated items and existing board copper avoided duplicate segments when merging stages. |
| `pair`, `length`, `tune` | One rejected differential-pair trial, repeated length reads, 18 single-net tuning operations in the two final tuning passes | Tuning placed small 45° corrections without manually moving the bends. I supplied the length deficit from both sides of each AC capacitor and used pin-to-pin measurements for Ethernet. |
| `cleanup` | Several trial and applied cleanup passes | One pass split 61 tee junctions, removed 14 dead ends and 31 redundant segments, and shortened copper by 49.488 mm. This made later connectivity inspection easier. |
| `silk --fix` | One main pass on all 216 movable reference fields | It moved 142 of the 204 overlapping references automatically. It identified every one of the remaining 62 and what blocked it. I placed those with a wider search and kept all fields visible. |
| `drc --baseline`, `check`, `same` | Several DRC comparisons, three aggregate-check attempts across candidate and final boards, and repeated placement comparisons | Enabling courtyards found two actual assembly overlaps at Y1. The final aggregate result distinguished zero KiCad opens, zero nets in pieces and zero bt findings from the 17 inherited DRC errors. `same` confirmed the locked placements and pad nets. |

The single batch route reduced the saved-board KiCad open count from 250 before the return-path changes to 107 after the return-path changes and batch work. Those totals cover the entire board and count different things from the 95 completed nets. There was no timed manual control run or token counter. Reporting seconds per saved manual route or an exact token saving would be speculative.

## Where the output needed interpretation

- **A length-matched pair can be physically uncoupled.** `bt pair … --nets PCIe_RX_P_2,PCIe_RX_N_2 --width .18 --gap .15 --layers F.Cu,B.Cu --tune` returned “ok,” zero skew, both tracks 25.03 mm, and “vias Infinity mm apart.” P stayed on the front with zero vias. N ran most of the way on the back with two vias. I discarded it. A differential-pair success criterion should check same-layer coupled length, maximum continuous uncoupled distance, and equal, adjacent transitions. The two layer itineraries and worst separation belong on the first line of the result.
- **Landing inside a zone outline can still leave a pad open after refill.** The plane tool quickly found legal copper and via locations. Tight reaches at the RP2040 and small plane islands still took a saved-board connectivity pass. For example, a local attempt could not reach three pads within 1.5 mm. `via --near R66.2 --radius 5` found a legal site 4.342 mm away. C78 and R67 ultimately needed new locations. A plane result that optionally verifies filled connectivity back to a named source pad would have saved iterations.
- **Dead branches can require repeated removal.** After `cleanup --dead`, KiCad still reported one-layer vias and dangling tracks. Removing a redundant via could expose the next segment as a dead end. I used KiCad’s connectivity graph, retained every previously connected pad, refilled, and repeated DRC until all such findings disappeared. Iterating this inside `cleanup` with a before/after pad-group check would help.
- **Some report limits look like changes to the board.** The starting KiCad JSON stopped at 499 unconnected items and 199 text-height warnings. Once the movable fields were enlarged, the baseline comparison called 16 unchanged fields “new.” Their UUIDs and geometry were unchanged. For slivers, `drc --baseline --courtyards` printed the count and several empty detail lines because KiCad returned no item positions. A truncation marker and a fallback location or highlighted region would make both reports easier to act on.
- **The aggregate checker exhausted the default Node stack.** `bt check` on the nearly final filled board exited 2 after 19 seconds with “Maximum call stack size exceeded.” On this installation, invoking the same module with a larger stack (`node --stack_size=48000 /opt/bt/bt.mjs check …`, with a larger shell stack limit) completed the audit. The final independent checks then agreed with KiCad. Large zone or connectivity lists should be handled iteratively. A partial result that identifies the failing phase would also help.
- **The inherited hole rule needed a separate audit.** The starting project allowed zero copper-to-hole clearance. A trial in KiCad at 0.20 mm found 79 pours and eight track segments too close to nonplated holes, plus four fixed USB-C pad-to-hole clearances. I changed the project rule, moved the affected low-speed tracks and refilled. Showing the actual minimum copper-to-NPTH clearance, including the zone edge, in the board summary would have surfaced this earlier.

## Work done outside bt

I used pcbnew for the floorplan so that each repeated converter block could preserve its input, switch-node, output and feedback geometry. I used a common-centerline search for the main PCIe, USB and Ethernet channels, and a detailed grid search between KiCad-connected copper groups for the remaining dense escapes. I also tested Freerouting on a copy. After more than twelve minutes it had produced no completed first-pass session, so none of its output was used.

For length sign-off I measured separate PCIe nets on both sides of series capacitors and traced Ethernet between specific component pins, with through-via travel included. A channel query accepting endpoints and a sequence of passives would remove much of that custom work. Reporting how much of a trace projects onto its intended adjacent reference plane would be more informative than total length alone. I would keep the small, read-only inspection commands and transactional output files as they are.
